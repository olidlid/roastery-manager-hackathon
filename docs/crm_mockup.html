Why these six numbers
Sales dashboards normally mix activity (calls, visits, meetings) with outcome (deals, volume). This app records no activity at all — there is no visit or call log — so every activity metric would have to be invented. It is left out on purpose.

What is left is what a roastery actually lives on: repeat business. For wholesale, retention metrics — reorder rate, order frequency, customers gone quiet — say more about a sales than raw volume does, because volume mostly reflects who was handed the bigger accounts.
Three fairness rules built into this tab
  • Each sales is compared with their own previous period, not with each other. Here both columns read "first full period" — ordering only really started in August, and 5,001 kg against 10 kg would be a meaningless number to show.
  • Per-customer numbers sit next to totals. 30 customers versus 9 is not a fair fight; 167 kg versus 59 kg per customer is a real comparison.
  • Failures that belong to production are not counted against sales. Unable to Fulfill happens when stock falls short — it appears under service quality, never in this scorecard.
If you later want activity measured too, the follow-up notes you mentioned for the CRM page are exactly the missing piece — once a note or a visit is logged, "customers touched this week" becomes real instead of guessed.

How the next order is estimated

Two methods run side by side, and each customer keeps the mix that has actually worked for them.

The two methods
1 · Rhythm — the median gap between this customer's own orders. masla orders every 7, 8, 6, 10 days → next one ≈ 8 days after the last.

2 · Consumption — how fast they burn coffee: kg per day from past orders, then "how long will the last order last?". A 100 kg order for a customer who burns 5 kg/day → ≈ 20 days.
Weighing them
Each time the page loads, both methods are replayed against that customer's own history, and the one that would have been closer gets the bigger share. Nothing is trained once and frozen, so as the history grows past a year, the mix moves on its own.

Today your customers do not order "when the coffee runs out" — jomo ordered 150 kg and came back the next day — so consumption on its own is three times worse. But the weighted mix, which gives each method the share it has earned per customer, edges out rhythm alone. A fixed 70/30 blend does not: the weighting has to be learned, not chosen.
Guardrails
  • Under 3 orders → no date. One gap is a coincidence, not a pattern.
  • 3–5 orders → rhythm only, with a wide ± range. From 6 orders the weighted mix switches on.
  • Clamped between half and 2.5× the customer's usual gap, so a 165-day estimate can never reach the screen.
  • Dormant customers get no estimate — "should have ordered 8 Jul" is noise, so the column shows how long they have been away instead.
  • The ± range comes from real error, not a guess: how far off this customer's own past estimates were.
  • Accuracy is published under the table, so if the method stops working you find out from the screen, not from a complaint.

Notes on the design

  • Two ways to filter, one table. The tiles on top are the quick view; the funnel on Category, Sales and Status is the precise one. The table stays the single place where work happens.
  • Sales ownership is management-only. Changing the sales here never changes what a sales may select on an order — category still decides that.
  • Every number is honest. Each figure comes from real orders: kg, bags, dates, coffee mix. Nothing is invented, and nothing is in currency.
  • The Journey tab is the gift. It reads like a profile, not a report — and the recap card can be printed and handed to the customer, which is what makes the roastery look like a partner who pays attention.
  • A sales row opens the same kind of modal a customer does. Same two-tab shape, same numbers, one level up — so a leader learns one screen and reads both. Its Customers tab is the handover list: every account that sales holds, who is due, who has gone quiet.
  • Milestones are computed, not typed. First order, records broken, coffee switches, anniversaries, comebacks after a long gap — all derived, so they keep appearing without anyone maintaining them.
